🔴 SonicWall SMA1000 Zero-Day Chain Under Active Exploitation — Chains to Unauthenticated RCE

🆕 CVE-2026-83548 (CVSS 10.0) & CVE-2026-83549 (CVSS 7.8): SSRF-to-Command-Injection Chain in SMA1000 Remote Access Appliances (Active Exploitation Confirmed; Hotfixes Shipped September 1, 2026)

SonicWall confirmed active exploitation of two vulnerabilities in its SMA1000 series secure remote-access appliances (models 6210, 7210, and 8200v) and released emergency hotfixes on September 1. CVE-2026-83548, rated a maximum CVSS 10.0, is a pre-authentication server-side request forgery flaw caused by an unintended alternate access path in the Appliance Work Place interface; it grants unauthenticated access to internal functionality. That access can then be chained into CVE-2026-83549 (CVSS 7.8), an OS command injection vulnerability in the Appliance Management Console, to execute operating system commands without any prior authentication. SonicWall's fixed builds are 12.4.3-03526 and 12.5.0-02952, and the vendor is urging affected customers to update immediately and investigate for signs of prior compromise.

PORTFOLIO IMPACT: SMA1000 appliances are exactly the class of internet-facing remote-access infrastructure MSPs deploy and manage for SMB clients, and a CVSS 10.0 unauthenticated RCE chain with confirmed in-the-wild exploitation is as urgent as this category of advisory gets. This is a direct, time-sensitive prompt for MDR/EDR account teams to confirm which managed clients run SMA1000 appliances, verify patch status against the September 1 hotfix builds, and check for indicators of prior compromise rather than assuming patching alone closes the window.

🔴 "FalconFlank" — Unpatched Privilege-Escalation Zero-Day Published Against CrowdStrike's Own EDR Sensor

🆕 Researcher Publishes Working PoC Turning Falcon Sensor's Macro-Removal Feature Into a SYSTEM-Privilege Escalation Path (PoC Published September 3, 2026; No CVE or Patch as of September 5)

Security researcher "Chaotic Eclipse" published working proof-of-concept code for a privilege-escalation flaw dubbed FalconFlank in CrowdStrike's Falcon Sensor for Windows. The exploit abuses a Falcon feature that automatically strips malicious macros from Microsoft Office documents — a remediation routine that runs with SYSTEM-level privileges — by using named-pipe manipulation, NTFS reparse points, and DLL injection to trick that privileged routine into acting on attacker-controlled content, delivering full SYSTEM access to a low-privileged attacker who already has a local foothold on the machine. As of September 3, CrowdStrike had not confirmed the flaw, assigned a CVE, or issued a CVSS score; a CrowdStrike spokesperson said the company is "actively investigating" and advised customers to disable the "Office File Suspicious Macro Removal" Windows policy setting as an interim workaround, while noting that Cloud Anti-malware protections for Microsoft Office files remain active regardless.

PORTFOLIO IMPACT: This is a security agent — not a general application — being turned into a local privilege-escalation path, which is a pointed reminder that EDR/AV sensors are trusted, high-privilege software and therefore high-value targets in their own right. Whether or not our own EDR is affected by this specific issue, it's a useful, dated example for internal conversations about sensor hardening and attack-surface review of our own agents, and a legitimate talking point in competitive deals where an unpatched, publicly disclosed flaw in a rival EDR agent is relevant.

🔴 Aesto Health Discloses Breach of 9.5 Million Patient Records Traced to Compromised AWS Infrastructure

🆕 Intrusion Detected December 2025, Fully Disclosed This Week: SSNs, Government IDs, and Health Data Exposed for 9,540,683 People

Healthcare technology company Aesto Health disclosed that attackers accessed a portion of its Amazon Web Services infrastructure between roughly December 2 and 18, 2025; the company detected the incident around December 18 but the notification and reporting process to HHS and affected individuals stretched on for months, with public disclosure and detailed press coverage concentrated this week. The exposed data set includes names, dates of birth, medical information, health insurance details, driver's license numbers, government-issued identification numbers, financial account information, taxpayer IDs, and — for some individuals — Social Security numbers. Aesto reported 9,540,683 affected individuals to HHS' Office for Civil Rights, making this the second-largest confirmed healthcare data breach disclosed so far this year, behind DentaQuest's roughly 15-million-record breach. Aesto says it has found no evidence to date of identity theft or financial fraud tied to the incident.

PORTFOLIO IMPACT: A cloud-infrastructure compromise at a healthcare vendor, rather than a ransomware encryption event, is a useful example for client conversations that conflate "we have EDR/MDR" with "our cloud infrastructure is covered" — this breach originated in AWS infrastructure, not an endpoint, and reinforces that cloud posture and identity/access management sit outside traditional endpoint and managed-detection coverage unless explicitly scoped in.

🟡 Manchester Airports Group: 640GB / 8.8 Million Customer Records Published After Failed Extortion

🆕 Root Cause Was an Exposed Admin API Key Sitting in Plain Sight in Frontend JavaScript, Not a Sophisticated Exploit (Data Published August 30 – September 3, 2026)

Extortion group FulcrumSec claimed responsibility on August 30 for a breach of Manchester Airports Group (MAG), which operates Manchester, London Stansted, and East Midlands airports, initially claiming roughly 86GB of compressed data. After MAG refused to pay, FulcrumSec published the stolen data starting September 3, and the extracted files reportedly total around 640GB covering approximately 8.8 million customers — email addresses, phone numbers, browser and device details, IP addresses and geolocation, purchase histories, vehicle registration plates, and names. According to researcher reporting, initial access came from admin API keys for customer-engagement platform Iterable that were left exposed in plain sight in the frontend JavaScript of all three MAG airport websites — not a zero-day or a phishing campaign, but a basic credential-hygiene failure.

PORTFOLIO IMPACT: This is a clean, concrete example of a breach that no amount of endpoint or DNS-layer tooling would have caught, because the exposure was a hardcoded credential shipped to every visitor's browser — the kind of gap that application-layer security awareness and secure-development practices catch, not traditional EDR/MDR telemetry. It's a useful client-conversation anchor for explaining what's in scope versus out of scope for our portfolio, and a reminder that "we have strong endpoint security" doesn't answer every attack-surface question a customer might have.

📌 Strategic Context: This Week the Security Tools Are the Target, and the Breaches Are Basic Hygiene Failures

Two distinct patterns stand out this week. First, the SonicWall and FalconFlank stories both put security-adjacent infrastructure — a remote-access appliance and an EDR sensor itself — in the crosshairs rather than a generic business application, continuing a theme from recent briefings (Cisco FMC, Cisco ASA/FTD, VMware vCenter) where the tools organizations rely on for security or connectivity are increasingly the vulnerability. The unpatched, publicly disclosed FalconFlank PoC against a leading EDR vendor's own sensor is a particularly sharp version of that pattern and worth watching for CrowdStrike's formal response. Second, the Aesto Health and Manchester Airports Group stories are both, at root, credential and access-management failures rather than novel exploitation techniques — a compromised AWS environment and a hardcoded API key in public-facing JavaScript, respectively — reinforcing that a large share of this year's biggest breaches trace back to basic hygiene gaps that traditional endpoint tooling doesn't address. Separately, this week also saw a positive development worth a brief mention: an international law-enforcement operation dismantled the Sality peer-to-peer botnet, which had operated for roughly 23 years, illustrating that patient, coordinated takedown efforts do work against even the most durable criminal infrastructure. PORTFOLIO IMPLICATION: Worth confirming with the MDR/EDR team whether current external-attack-surface monitoring would catch an exposed API key in client-facing JavaScript (the Manchester Airports root cause) — that's a gap category distinct from what endpoint telemetry typically surfaces, and a good prompt for whether it belongs in scope for account teams.
